End to end: why small companies can’t afford handoffs

2026-10-20 ·7 min ·Architecture

Small businessArchitectureProductOperations

In a large company, a feature passes through five pairs of hands before it reaches a user. In a small company it usually passes through one. What that person needs most is the ability to carry a problem from "what does this person need?" all the way to "is it still working six months later?", and writing the code is only one part of that.

I've worked at both ends. At Mercado Livre I was one of eight engineers on a simulation platform used by around five thousand supply-chain specialists, with product, data and platform teams around us. At Capellaris, an agentic platform built for small and medium businesses, I was the only engineer. My business partner handled the commercial and financial side, and everything technical, from the first conversation with a pilot customer to post-sales support, was mine. Both setups work, and they need different kinds of engineers.

Five jobs

Delivering something end to end means doing five jobs. In a large organisation each one has its own title:

  1. Understanding the need. What the user is trying to get done, which isn't always what they asked for.
  2. Technical requirements. Volumes, budget, data, integrations, and what happens when it fails.
  3. Architecture. The shape of the system, chosen for the team that will run it.
  4. Implementation. The code.
  5. Maintenance. Operating it, fixing it, and changing it as the business moves.

Each handoff between those jobs loses some context. A large company covers the loss with headcount, documents and meetings, and it can afford to. A small company has no product manager to translate the customer, no SRE to absorb the operational cost of a clever design, and often no second engineer to question the first one's assumptions. Wherever it does hand work off, some of the original intent gets lost on the way.

Work back from the request

Users describe solutions, because that's the vocabulary most people have for a problem. The engineer's job is to work back from the request to whatever it stands for.

At Buscaônibus, a travel meta-search platform, five small bus companies had no digital integration at all. There was nothing on their side to connect to and nobody there to build it, so a normal integration was off the table. What the business needed was for their routes to show up in search like everyone else's. Put that way, the answer was on-demand scrapers on Cloud Run and a monitoring dashboard that flagged when a company stopped returning data.

The Capellaris pilots were smaller still: an independent consultancy, a restaurant and an art atelier. For one of them, what mattered was a single view of financial planning across departments, to replace fragmented manual processes. Agents were how I built it. If I'd started from "what can agents do?" instead of "where does this business lose track of its plan?", I'd have ended up with a demo.

This works at any scale. On the Mercado Livre simulators, splitting one large input spreadsheet into smaller sequential steps cut input errors by 17%. That needed no model and no new service, only a closer look at how people were filling the spreadsheet in.

Requirements nobody writes down

Small companies rarely write technical requirements down, because it isn't anyone's job. The requirements exist anyway, and they come due later. Before I design anything, I ask:

  • Who uses it, and how often? Ten people once a week and ten thousand people an hour need different systems.
  • What happens if it's down for an hour, or for a day? The honest answer is often "not much", and knowing that saves a lot of money.
  • What data does it touch, and who owns that data? This gets more pressing once an LLM sits in the path.
  • What does it cost per month at today's volume, and at ten times that?
  • Who fixes it at two in the morning? In a small company I let this answer shape all the others.

Architecture for the team you have

In a small company, most architecture decisions turn out to be operations decisions. Every service, queue and datastore is something someone has to understand, upgrade, secure and pay for, so the design that looks best on a whiteboard is rarely the right one.

Capellaris isn't a small system. It's multi-tenant, with a full RAG pipeline, hybrid search and hierarchical multi-agent orchestration. It still runs on managed services: Cloud Run and Firebase on GCP, Supabase PostgreSQL with pgvector, and Cloudflare in front. The vectors sit in the same Postgres as the rest of the data instead of in a separate vector database, and the models are behind one interface so no single vendor owns the product. I made those choices so that one engineer could run the whole thing.

For a small company, I pick the architecture that the smallest realistic team could still run at two in the morning.

Implementation got cheaper

Writing code is what people picture when they hear "engineer", and it's the stage that has changed most. I use AI coding tools every day, and they've made implementation cheaper. As I argued in AI is an accelerator, not a decision-maker, the decisions around the code haven't got any cheaper, so they're now a bigger share of the job. When code was slow to write, fuzzy requirements had time to surface before much was built on them. Now you can build the wrong thing in an afternoon.

Maintenance tests whether you understood

Launch is roughly the middle of the work. Most of what I know about building systems I learned in the months after they shipped.

At Stormtech I wrote about half of a set of RPA bots that scraped bank portals to update loan status across 18 integrated banks, and I maintained all of them, which meant updating a bot every time a bank changed its portal. Maintaining your own code next to other people's shows you fast which assumptions were fragile. It's also why I built a dashboard of bot health (running, failed, unused), which cut the time to spot a broken pipeline from about two hours to under one.

At Buscaônibus I was the sole technical owner of a backend handling millions of daily searches, so on-call and incident response were mine along with the rest of the production lifecycle. Knowing you'll be the one paged changes how you build. You put feature flags with immediate rollback in place before you need them; that's the approach behind the platform migration I described in Planning a legacy migration. You give each data source its own cache TTL and build a tool to inspect the keys, because you'll be the one inspecting them.

A quick test

Ask whoever built a system how they'd find out it was broken. If the answer is "a user would tell us", they didn't expect to maintain it.

Maintenance feeds the next requirement

When one person owns all five stages, what they learn in maintenance goes straight back into understanding the need. Whoever hears the complaint can change the requirement, the architecture or the code without a ticket crossing three teams first.

On Capellaris, users rated every project the manager agent delivered on a one-to-five star scale. I tracked that rating from the first single-agent deliveries through to hierarchical multi-agent runs, and later projects scored higher and needed fewer refinement rounds. I put that down to a short loop: the person reading the ratings was also deciding what to build next.

Where end to end goes wrong

One person owning everything has its own failure modes, and ignoring them is how a small company ends up depending on a single engineer.

  • Knowledge that lives in one head is a risk to the company. Owning something end to end includes writing it down and choosing technology someone else can pick up.
  • Nobody is equally strong at all five stages. Part of the skill is knowing which one is your weakest and when to bring in a specialist.
  • Past a certain size, a single owner slows the company down. At that point the job is to hand stages off on purpose, with their context.
  • It doesn't guarantee a product. At ReciclaComigo, a five-person startup, I built a reverse vending machine's edge software, its Arduino firmware and its AWS environment, and the company closed before public launch.

If you're hiring for a small company

Besides asking candidates what they built, ask what they kept running, what broke, and what they changed because of it. The answers show whether someone has done the whole loop or only the middle of it.

If you're an engineer and want this skill, stay with something after it ships and be the person who gets the call. It's the least glamorous of the five stages, and it's taught me more about the other four than anything else.
